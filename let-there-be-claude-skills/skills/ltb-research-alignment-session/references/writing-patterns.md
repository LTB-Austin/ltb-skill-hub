# Writing patterns

How the variable screens are written in the reference builds. Use the pattern, not the wording.

---

## The challenge

Three moves: the shift or problem, what the client wants, and our job inside it.

> Millions of Americans should not be taking NSAIDs. Tylenol's largest competitors are Advil and private-label ibuprofen. The brief is to build a substantiated, science-backed case for why people with certain conditions should not be reaching for ibuprofen, so that those people consider Tylenol instead.
>
> Kenvue has called this the Special Condition Consumer opportunity. The strategy has existed inside the business for years. Our job is to help build the evidence base underneath it.

> The modern nicotine user looks different than they did 40 years ago. … Your current claims are shared across the NRT category, which leaves little room to set Habitrol® apart. This project is about new claims, not new messaging.

Notes: use the client's name for the opportunity. Name the competitor plainly. State the business objective in their words. A rhetorical question is acceptable once, only if it is the client's own framing.

## Why evidence, not positioning

Name what makes the argument hard: it compares against a competitor or class, and it speaks to a sensitive population. Then tie it to the client's own stated standard.

> The argument compares Tylenol® with a competing drug class, and it speaks to people who have a diagnosed condition. That combination is what makes it hard to approve and valuable to own.

> You told us clinically proven is the gold standard, reputable sources are the minimum for use claims, and Habitrol® does not make comparative competitive claims today, but would like to. There is no claims matrix yet.

## Stats and the pattern underneath

Four figures, each with a short plain label that says exactly what it counts. Then two observations that connect the figures into an insight the client may not have drawn themselves.

> 86% of Americans with diabetes also have hypertension. Over half of diagnosed diabetics also have arthritis.

> Across the August 2026 condition profiles, self-reported "NSAIDs contraindicated" runs 12% to 20% within each condition group, well below clinical prevalence.

The second example is the model: two of their numbers set side by side to reveal a gap. Always name the source documents.

## Approved language: "There is room to say more"

Respect what is in market, then name two specific places evidence can add. One is usually *explaining* what the claim only asserts. The other is usually a population or behavior the client's data shows that the claim set does not reach.

> Acetaminophen is among the most studied actives in the world. The approved language establishes the position. The same evidence base can also explain what changes for someone who has the condition.
>
> They are written condition by condition. Your own data says most of this audience manages more than one condition. That opens a line the current set does not cover yet.

## Search priorities

Each priority: a name, a size or rationale line, and the lines of inquiry written as what we will look for, not what we will find. Rank them, and say what the ranking rests on.

> **Priority 2 — Renal, including the undiagnosed** · 5.6MM diagnosed · 33.1MM undiagnosed CKD
> Your own data puts undiagnosed CKD at 33.1MM, and diuretic users at 13.4MM. The documented interaction between NSAIDs, diuretics and blood pressure medication is a specific mechanism that can be explained to a consumer, and the current claim set does not cover it.

Where the priority rests on general knowledge rather than their data, keep it conditional:

> Published prescribing guidance for older adults treats systemic NSAIDs as a caution in its own right, independent of any single diagnosis. If that holds up, the argument stops being a niche condition play and becomes a case about a very large population.

Also map the counter-argument as part of the search where it exists:

> FDA now lets certain pouches and authorized vapes be presented as lower-risk for adult smokers who switch completely. We map that, so the argument is built knowing the counter.

## Exclusions

Each one gets a reason. The strongest reasons:

- **It invites the counter-argument.** "Acetaminophen carries its own hepatic cautions, and building an argument in a category where the product has a labeled limitation invites the counter-argument."
- **The real number is smaller than it looks.** "NSAID-exacerbated respiratory disease affects a minority within that group, so the addressable number is much smaller than 20.3MM implies."
- **The competitor wins there.** "NSAIDs are effective for inflammatory pain, so an efficacy comparison is not the argument to make here. We propose treating arthritis as a population that carries other conditions, rather than a territory of its own."
- **It is already established.** "Already established on your label. We pull efficacy evidence only where it carries a new claim."
- **It concedes the frame.** "Competing on count concedes the category's own frame."
- **It is not where the switching is happening.** "7% of recent buyers prefer them."

Tag each `Proposed out` unless the SOW already excludes it (`Out of scope`). Offer a way back in where fair: "We propose parking it unless the literature comes back stronger than expected."

## Three directions

Three distinct shapes of argument, ordered so the risk spread is visible:

1. **The bold comparative one.** Names what the competitor does not do. Say it carries the most regulatory difficulty and why.
2. **The behavior or population insight.** Built on something in the client's data (stacking, the stall, the undiagnosed). It often rests on a body of literature rather than a head-to-head comparison.
3. **The one that argues for the brand's own path.** Moves the argument to the moment of choice or the brand's idea, rather than against a competitor. Least comparative risk. Name the open question it depends on.

Short names, plain: "The stack." "The stall." "Quit, don't switch." "On your terms." "Nobody ever told you to ask."

If the client's own materials pull two ways, put it to them:

> Your proposal said "quit, don't switch." Your brand book says "reduce on your own terms." Which promise is the platform built on?

## Questions for today

Each question must change the research depending on the answer. Give the reason where it is not obvious.

> NSAIDs as a class, or ibuprofen specifically? The two lead to different literature and different claims, so this shapes the research from day one.

> Which SKUs are in scope? Dose, form and duration all change what the literature supports, so the search follows the SKU.

> How far can the argument go? Is this a case for acetaminophen as the appropriate choice, or a case against ibuprofen? Where is your line?

> Source threshold. Which sources your medical, legal and regulatory team will accept: peer-reviewed only, or FDA, CDC and NIH positions as well.

> Reviewers. Who sits on Medical, Legal and Regulatory review for this work. Consolidated feedback comes through [name].

Also good: "Why now? The strategy has existed for years. What moved it forward?" and "What is the outcome you want at [business moment]? Directions to argue for, or a validated story to fund."

## Timeline side note

Anchor to the business moment, name what they will have in hand, and name the date that holds the plan together.

> The aim is the claims and a tested Science Story™ in hand for January quitting season. Your review process runs Brand first, then Medical, Regulatory and Legal, at about two weeks a round. This plan holds to two rounds. The final round runs across the holidays. Reviewer availability in late December is the date to confirm today.
