# Copy Craft — how LTB claims and Science Stories are written

<!-- Identical copies live in ltb-science-intelligence/references and ltb-science-story/references. Edit both. -->

The writing is the product. A claim goes in front of MLR and then in front of a consumer; a Science Story goes into a test where three hundred people read it and score it against two others. Nobody in either room sees the workbook. They see the sentence. A first draft that needs the strategist to rewrite every line has failed even if every citation is right.

This file is derived from LTB's own decks (Rocco & Roxie, VetIQ, Coppertone Sport, Ryaltris, Motrin). The examples are working language from those projects: use them to learn the shape, never reuse them for another client. The voice block in SKILL.md is the floor for everything here. Consumer-facing registers are allowed to move off that floor in exactly the ways this file describes, and no further.

## 1. The registers, and what each one is for

| Register | Reader | Job | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer claim | a shopper, in two seconds | make one true thing land, in their language | 1–2 short sentences, ≤12 words each; comparison not measurement; product named or paired with a brand line |
| HCP claim | prescriber, vet, pharmacist, rep | give a reason to recommend that survives a skeptical read | one precise sentence; INN names; endpoint, comparator, and direction stated; magnitude in the evidence line |
| MLR / internal expression | reviewer | show everything: what was measured, in whom, at what dose, what it can't say | full sentence plus evidence note plus guardrail; no compression |
| Evidence statement | anyone | state a finding and stop | the fact, its source, no product |
| Context (category) claim | consumer | make the problem real before the product appears | editorial fact about the body, the category, the moment; no product benefit implied |
| Bridge claim | consumer | join a moment to a formula action | two halves, each independently true; the join is the risk |
| Positioning line | consumer | close the story | the concept in one line, product named; rests on the whole evidence base |

Never mix registers in one line. A consumer claim with a p-value in it is an MLR expression that leaked; an HCP claim written as "helps support" is a consumer claim that lost its nerve.

## 2. Consumer claim lines

**Shape.** One or two sentences. Often a fragment and a turn: the first beat states a fact or a tension the reader recognizes, the second beat turns it.

> You stop smelling your home. Your guests don't.
> Odor sinks into carpet and cushions. Then it comes back.
> Same SPF. Not the same coverage. That's what CoverGuard™ is for.
> Two pain relievers, side by side. Only one works on the inflammation.
> One dose. Up to 6 hours of relief. Then get on with your day.

**Sentence mechanics.**
- Periods, not commas. Fragments are allowed when the rhythm needs them.
- Second person where the claim is about the reader's experience ("your dog," "your guests," "you reach for").
- One idea per line. If a second idea arrives, it is a second claim.
- Concrete nouns from the reader's life: couch, rug, dog bed, class, shift, the turn, the cabinet. Not "surfaces," "daily activities," "environments."
- Verbs do the work; adjectives almost never appear. "Captures," "neutralizes," "beat," "lowered," "helps keep," "supports," "maintains," "goes after," "keeps working." Not "powerful," "advanced," "superior," "clinically-proven."
- Hedges are kept when the evidence needs them, and they are chosen deliberately: "about," "can," "up to," "nearly," "most." A guardrail that says *keep "about"* is a copy instruction, not a legal footnote.

**Product naming.** Three legal patterns:
1. Product performs the action in the sentence: "Motrin goes after what is sending it."
2. Fact, then product as the answer: "Skin isn't smooth. Sunscreen has to settle into it. Coppertone® Sport is formulated to go on evenly anyway."
3. Fact stands alone as a context claim and the brand line sits in separate copy. Required whenever the fact is prevalence or epidemiology: "71% of women under 25 get period cramp pain" must never read as "Motrin reduces missed class."

**Qualifiers match the evidence.** "Clinically shown" needs a human RCT on that endpoint. "Clinically studied" needs a human study. "Helps support / maintain" needs mechanism plus human data. "Proven" is never used.

**Comparatives name the molecule, not the brand.** "Acetaminophen, never Tylenol." "A steroid nose spray," not the competitor. If the comparison comes from two labels rather than a head-to-head trial, the footnote says so every time it appears.

## 3. Turning a measurement into a consumer sentence

The rule from the VetIQ work applies to every project: **the measurement stays in the substantiation file; the consumer hears the comparison.** No percentages to convert, no units, no decimals, no clinical terms.

| What was measured | What the consumer hears | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Forelimb peak force 106.68% of body weight at a trot, n=43 | "At a trot, one front leg carries more than your dog's whole body weight." | a comparison to something they can picture; the exact figure is in the note |
| 27–34 N/kg forelimb, jump-down from platform | "Jumping down off the couch lands with about three times that force." | everyday scene; "about" kept because the range is a range |
| 39.8% radiographic change, dogs 8 months–4 years | "Nearly 4 in 10 dogs under four already show joint changes." | 4 in 10, not 39.8%; "already" carries the timing; "joint changes," never the disease |
| 10 of 14 owners reported no signs, n=30 | "Most owners had no idea anything had changed." | small n, so no percentage in consumer copy at all |
| 62% vs 9% maintained daily activity, n=100 | "7x as many young women kept their day moving on Motrin than on acetaminophen." | ratio of proportions, derivation shown in the note (62 ÷ 9 = 6.9); "up to 7x" expected after MLR |
| ≥50% pain relief in 54% of participants | "For more than half the people who took it, at least half the pain — gone." | "more than half" beats 54%; note carries the dose disclosure |
| 34% used rescue medication vs 79% placebo | "Two out of three people didn't need anything else." | inverted to the positive, as a simple fraction |
| 15 min onset vs up to 12 h, from two labels | "First relief in 15 minutes instead of up to 12 hours — up to 48x sooner." | the multiple is shown with its arithmetic and its "label-to-label, not head-to-head" footnote |
| 60–80% less muscle force broke the joint down | not used as a figure; muscle is "your dog's shock absorber" | some numbers should never reach a consumer; use the idea instead |
| In vitro SPF 5.6 to 85.0 across seven products with identical filters | "Same filters. Same percentages. Fifteen times apart." | one clean ratio, no decimals; category statement, no brand |

**Rules that fall out of the table:**
- Percentages become fractions or comparisons: 4 in 10, more than half, two out of three, nearly all.
- Ratios come with their derivation in the evidence note, never in the line.
- Medians, subgroups, small samples, and dose-dependence become footnotes or "up to," and the claim card says which.
- Absolute and relative are both in the note; the line uses whichever the reader can picture, usually the comparison.
- If the number only exists in a subgroup, in vitro, or in a different population, the line says so or doesn't use the number.
- Some figures are for HCP and internal use only (disease prevalence gaps, mortality, injury odds). The claim library marks them "vet-facing / internal" and they never sit next to a product benefit.

## 4. HCP claim lines

The HCP reader is skeptical by training and short on time. The line has to survive someone who knows the literature, so it is precise where the consumer line is plain.

> Significant improvement in nasal symptom relief versus either olopatadine or mometasone furoate monotherapy, or placebo.
> RYALTRIS delivered significantly greater ocular symptom relief than mometasone furoate alone.
> A nasal corticosteroid on its own can take up to 12 hours to start working, and one to two weeks for maximum benefit.
> Cartilage matrix synthesis is regulated by the pattern of mechanical load, not merely its presence — which is why joint support belongs in a routine rather than a response.

**Shape.** One sentence. Names the molecule (INN), the endpoint, the comparator, and the direction. The magnitude, CI, p-value, n, and trial name go in the evidence line directly beneath: "Pooled rTOSS LSMD −0.27 (p=0.0037); replicated in the China Phase 3."

**What changes from consumer.**
- "Significant" is used, and means statistically significant.
- Mechanism is stated in the proper terms: "blocks histamine at the receptor," "COX inhibition lowers prostaglandin synthesis," not "quiets the pain."
- Comparators are explicit: monotherapy, placebo, the reference product's label.
- Label-derived facts are attributed to the label: "From the FDA-approved label for the reference spray. A statement of its own labelling, not an efficacy comparison."
- Inferences are marked as inferences. If the sentence contains "which is why," the card says: present as rationale, not as a finding.
- The reason-to-recommend can be stated as a consequence the prescriber cares about: "Prescribing one of them is prescribing less than what was tested."

**What does not change.** Still one idea per line. Still no adjectives doing the persuading. Still no "proven."

## 5. Context, bridge, and positioning claims

The Science Story usually needs three kinds of claim the workbook does not produce on its own. All three still trace to Study IDs.

**Context claims** make the problem real before the product appears. Editorial facts about the body, the category, or the moment.

> Every run, turn and landing is work for your dog's joints.
> Pet smells aren't people smells. They need a different fix.
> Your nose picks up pet odor in tiny amounts.

Rules: no product, no benefit implied, no disease named. The guardrail defines the safe register ("work, load and effort," never "damage, wear out, harm"). Prevalence figures live here and stay out of the same sentence as the brand.

**Bridge claims** join a moment to a formula action. Highest creative value, highest care.

> Every leap for the ball is muscle under load. VetIQ Hip & Joint helps maintain the muscle that carries it.
> The corner your dog takes at full speed puts two thirds more force through that leg. VetIQ Hip & Joint helps keep the joint lubricated and cushioned through it.

Rules: two sentences, first the moment, second the product. Each half independently supported by its own Study IDs; the join is what MLR reads. The verb in the second half is the structure/function verb the class permits. "Through," "during," and "for" are the safe prepositions; the product never reduces the force, prevents the injury, or repairs the tissue.

**Positioning lines** close the story. The concept in one sentence with the product named.

> Play is the load. VetIQ Hip & Joint is the daily support.
> Two different tools. Choose Motrin.
> Decide once, not all day. Motrin.
> Fifteen minutes, not twelve hours. Ask your doctor about RYALTRIS.

Rules: rests on the whole evidence base, which the card says plainly. Goes to MLR "as a theme, not as a substantiated efficacy statement." Keep "support," never "protection" or "defence." Usually a callback to the title or the opening line.

## 6. Evidence statements are not claims

Some findings are too useful to leave in the workbook and too raw to be claims. They are written as **evidence statements**: the finding, its source, and a full stop. No product.

> Active people get sunburned more than everyone else. (Holman 2018, 37.9% vs 32.8%, n=31,162)
> Same filters. Same percentages. Fifteen times apart, in Coppertone's own published research.
> The landmark trials behind sunscreen's protective evidence tested daily, consistent use, not occasional use.

They sit in their own section, labeled "evidence, not claim language," and they substantiate the claims rather than compete with them. Keeping them separate is what stops a category finding being read as a brand claim.

## 7. What travels with the line: the claim card

A claim is never presented as a bare sentence. The card around it is what lets a client and an MLR reviewer trust it, and writing the card is how the copywriter checks the line.

```
[TYPE · REGISTER · RISK]   or   [STATUS: IN-MARKET / SHORTLIST / NEW / APPROVED / PENDING]
CLM-nn
"The claim, verbatim, in quotes."
Evidence   Two or three plain sentences: what was measured, in whom, the figure with its
           context, and anything the line depends on (dose, timing, subgroup, median).
Source     Author, Year, Journal · Study ID(s)
Guardrail  The specific words this line may not use and why, and any word it must keep.
Category claim / Product claim / Product testing required   (whichever applies)
Alternates held   (optional) other wordings the client or we have written, kept for MLR.
```

**The evidence sentence is also copy.** It is written for a reader, not pasted from the abstract: "Two weeks of living with a smell raises your own threshold for detecting it and lowers how strong it seems. The effect held for up to two weeks after the exposure stopped." Same voice as the claim, one register more detailed.

**The guardrail is a wording rule, not a warning.** Good guardrails name words: *Keep "about."* *Say "can," not "does."* *"Joint changes," never osteoarthritis, in consumer copy.* *Never restores or replenishes joint fluid.* *Pain only, never fever.* *Consumer copy says "liquid softgel," never "solubilized."* A guardrail that says "use with caution" is not finished.

**Status tags are the client's vocabulary.** IN-MARKET (approved wording already in use), SHORTLIST (from the set they selected), NEW (ours), APPROVED, PENDING. Verbatim client wording is shown as they wrote it and labeled "shown verbatim." Never silently edit an approved line.

## 8. The sharper version

For lead claims, write two: the working line, and the version that would go on a pack, an end-frame, or a display. Then say what changed in one sentence.

> Working: "Same SPF. Not the same coverage. That's what CoverGuard™ is for."
> Sharper: "SPF is the number. CoverGuard™ is the difference."
> What changed: the number and the difference, in eight words, with the technology named rather than left to the lockup. No competitor named.

> Working: "CoverGuard™ Technology helps your sunscreen go on even and stay even, so it keeps protecting. Only with Coppertone® Sport."
> Sharper: "CoverGuard™ goes on even, stays even, keeps protecting."
> What changed: all three verbs now describe the film, so the third clause completes the sequence instead of switching subject.

The sharper version is where the writing gets tested hardest: fewer words, same truth, no new claim. If compressing it added a promise, it isn't sharper, it's a different claim.

## 9. Science Story concept craft: title, anchor, narrative

A Science Story is a **point of view** on the evidence, not a benefit statement. Three directions in a test must differ in the *problem they open on*, so the test measures which point of view moves people, not which adjective.

### Title

Two to five words. A named strategic angle the brand could hold for a season.

> The Guest Test · Built For This Smell · Odor Lives In Two Places
> Two Different Tools · One Decision · Keeps You in Motion · The First Hour
> The Science of Play · Prescribe the Combination, Not the Component · Up to 48x Sooner

What they have in common: a noun phrase or short imperative; names a tension or reframes the category; no adjectives; no pun; you can say what job it does in one breath. What they never are: a tagline ("Feel the difference"), a benefit ("Fast relief"), or a description of the science ("The Film-Former Story").

A quick test for distinctness: write each title's job in one line. If two jobs read the same, you have two executions of one direction, not two directions.

> The Guest Test → building trust in something invisible
> Built For This Smell → recruiting from the general cleaning aisle
> Odor Lives In Two Places → growing basket size and arming the sales team

### Narrative anchor

One or two sentences, written for the brand team, stating what the direction *does for the brand* and the move it makes.

> Makes the invisible mechanism matter by pointing out the consumer's own sensory blind spot — without ever suggesting they've done something wrong.
> Duration told as decisions instead of hours. The measure of Motrin is how many times pain gets to interrupt the day.
> Motrin's proof is spread across occasions nobody connects. Motion is what links them, so four separate proofs read as one brand.

The anchor is strategic, not consumer copy. It is where the "why this direction" lives so the narrative doesn't have to carry it. Optionally add **Where this could go** (three or four fragments on the creative it opens) and, where relevant, **Why this sits outside the test**.

### Narrative

The narrative is what gets tested. It is written the way the consumer would hear it, and it follows a shape that every LTB story has used:

**Beat 1 — Open inside the reader's experience.** A concrete moment they recognize but have never had named. Second person. Short sentences. No product yet.

> Live with a dog or a cat long enough and your nose stops registering them. Nothing has gone wrong.
> The pain starts small. Barely there at nine, worse by ten. By eleven you've had the same argument with yourself four times.
> You take your nose spray. Then you wait.
> Two pain relievers sit on the same shelf, an arm apart. Nobody ever explained the difference, so you reach for the name you already know.

**Beat 2 — Reframe.** The thing nobody said. The science in plain words: what's actually happening, why the obvious answer doesn't work.

> A scent spray puts a smell on top of the one you have — the pet odor is still there at full strength, now with perfume over it.
> When your period starts, the lining of your uterus releases prostaglandins. They squeeze the muscle down on its own blood supply, and that squeeze is the cramp.

**Beat 3 — The product enters, by name, doing one specific thing.** Same register. The number, if any, in its consumer form. Disclaimer asterisks and footnote markers carried.

> Rocco & Roxie Pet Odor Eliminator is built for the pet smells you've stopped noticing. Dual Action captures the odor molecules in the air, then neutralizes what's left in the couch, the rug, the dog bed.
> One dose of Motrin ends the argument. In studies, more than half of people had at least half their pain gone — and one dose covers up to six hours.

**Beat 4 — Consequence in the reader's life.** What changes. Often a triad of concrete outcomes.

> So the practice happens. The shift gets worked. The plans hold.
> Six hours is a shift. A day of class. An afternoon that goes the way you planned it.

**Beat 5 — Close on the positioning line.** The title or opening called back, product named.

> So the next time someone says your place smells great, they mean it.
> Play is the load. VetIQ Hip & Joint is the daily support.
> Decide once, not all day. Motrin.

**Length is set by the medium, not by a default.** A deck stimulus runs 90–180 words. A 30-second video script runs 75–90. A 60-second HCP script runs 150–200. State the target before writing. Narratives compared in one test stay within about 20% of each other in length so consumers compare ideas, not word counts.

**Mechanics.** Periods over commas; no semicolons; fragments for rhythm; triads for concreteness ("the couch, the rug, the dog bed"); numbers only in consumer form; comparator by molecule not brand; the product named at least once by full name; nothing in the narrative that is not a Claim ID or plainly positioning copy.

**The hook is the recognizable tension, not a clever line.** Every strong LTB opening has been a true thing the reader already half-knows: your nose stops noticing, you argue with yourself about whether the pain is bad enough, you were handed whatever was in the cabinet. Wordplay, puns, and rhetorical questions are still outside the voice. What's allowed is putting the reader in a moment.

**Every factual sentence maps to a Claim ID.** List them under the narrative. Sentences that don't map are positioning copy and are reviewed as such. Prevalence and epidemiology generally stay on the claim card and out of the narrative; if a figure appears in the narrative, it carries a footnote marker to the substantiation.

### The compare slide

When there are several directions, the side-by-side has fixed rows: **Anchor · Best for · Evidence · Runs today · Watch for**. "Watch for" is the honest risk of each direction in one sentence ("Pushed too far it argues an air spray alone can't finish the job"). The slide says "these are not ranked" and then says what each one does best. The client chooses.

## 10. Working with the client's priority claims

After the client reacts, their preferred claims come back and the story has to absorb them. Every one of them is accounted for, visibly.

**The mapping slide.** One row per client claim: their wording · the concept it lands in · Claim ID · status. Statuses are exactly these:

- **Carried** — as written, or "lightly tuned" with the tune named ("general-pain scope").
- **Consolidated** — two claims from the same source become one stronger headline; both originals held as alternates.
- **Replaced** — a stronger figure from the same evidence base does the same job; the reason is stated ("rescue-medication figure replaces the duration claim; not a duration claim").
- **Held** — kept out, with the evidence reason ("dental-only evidence, outside this concept").

**"What carried over."** Per direction, a short slide: FROM [direction 1] / FROM [direction 2] / NEW THIS ROUND. Nothing the client liked is dropped silently.

**Optimizing a client claim.** Legitimate moves, all of which stay on the same Study IDs:
- Scope it to what the evidence supports ("pain, never fever").
- Render the same number in a more legible form (responder rate in place of NNT; "two out of three" in place of 66%).
- Consolidate when one source reports both figures.
- Write the fallback alongside the lead ("the fallback if the multiple does not clear MLR").
- Mark the legal flag and keep it marked through every revision.

Not legitimate: strengthening a qualifier, adding a magnitude, widening a population, or dropping a hedge the guardrail says to keep. Any of those creates a derived claim (`CLM-nnD`), and it goes back to Science Intelligence for scoring.

## 11. Vocabulary: the safe register and the closed words

Guardrails are project-specific, but the same pairs recur across every LTB project. Default to the left column; the right column needs a study that directly supports the stronger word and an MLR read.

| Safe register | Closed without direct support |
|---|---|
| helps keep, supports, maintains | restores, repairs, rebuilds, replenishes |
| work, load, effort | damage, wear out, harm, injury |
| joint changes, discomfort | osteoarthritis, disease names, diagnoses |
| support, care, routine | protection, defence, prevention |
| proactive, before anything shows | preventative, stops X from happening |
| clinically studied / shown (as evidence allows) | clinically proven, proven |
| works on the inflammation | targets it at the source |
| beat [molecule] on pain | stronger than [brand], more powerful |
| absorbs faster, peak absorption in about half an hour | works in minutes, instant relief |
| up to, about, nearly, most | always, every, all, guaranteed |
| covers up vs gets rid of | eliminates all, 100% |

Words that are simply out, in every register: proven, revolutionary, breakthrough, cutting-edge, powerful, superior (unless head-to-head data), miracle, magic, gem.

## 12. Self-edit: the tells that a line needs another pass

Read every consumer line against these before it goes in the card. If any is true, rewrite.

- There is a percentage with a decimal, a unit, or a p-value in it.
- Two ideas are in one sentence.
- An adjective is doing the persuading.
- The product is named in the same sentence as a prevalence statistic.
- The qualifier is stronger than the study design ("shown" on a review; "clinically" on an in vitro result).
- A hedge the guardrail says to keep has been trimmed.
- The comparator is a brand name.
- A number appears whose derivation is not in the evidence note.
- The line could be swapped into a competitor's ad without changing a word (no product action, no specific mechanism, no specific moment).
- Read aloud, it sounds like a label rather than a person.

For a narrative, additionally:

- The product appears before the reader's moment does.
- Any sentence would need a semicolon to be grammatical.
- The close is not a callback to the title or opening.
- The three directions in a test open on the same problem.
- Two narratives in the same test differ in length by more than about 20%.
- A factual sentence has no Claim ID under it.
- You can't state in one line what job this direction does that the others don't.
