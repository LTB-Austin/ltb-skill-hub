# The MOA Walkthrough — writing the science so animators can visualize it

This is the core of the Science 101. Everything else is context; this section is what the creative team actually builds from. Write it as if the reader is a talented visual artist with no biology background who has to draw this next week.

## The standard the section must meet

An animator should be able to read the walkthrough once and sketch a rough sequence without asking what anything means. If they'd have to look up a word or guess at scale, the writing isn't done.

Three failure modes to avoid:

- **Textbook voice** — accurate but unvisualizable ("competitive inhibition at the receptor site"). Say what moves, what blocks what, and what changes as a result.
- **Vague hand-waving** — "it supports healthy function." That gives an animator nothing to draw.
- **Overreach** — inventing a step to make the sequence flow. If the literature doesn't demonstrate a step, label it as inferred or leave it out.

## Structure: the mechanism as beats

Break the mechanism into **numbered sequential beats** — typically 4–7. A beat is one discrete thing that happens. If a beat can't be drawn as a distinct visual moment, it's either two beats or none.

For every beat, give all four:

1. **What happens** — the action, in plain declarative language.
2. **Where it happens** — the anatomical location, specific ("lining of the small intestine," not "in the body").
3. **What it looks like** — the visual consequence: what moves, what changes color or shape, what's crowded or clear, what's fast or slow.
4. **Evidence status** — demonstrated in the literature, or inferred. Mark it.

A worked example of the shape (illustrative only — never reuse this content):

> **Beat 3 — The active binds its target**
> *What happens:* The active molecule attaches to receptors on the cell surface, occupying the sites the irritant would otherwise use.
> *Where:* Surface of the epithelial cells lining the nasal passage.
> *What it looks like:* Scattered molecules drift in and lock onto surface sites; the sites fill one by one until the surface is largely occupied. Irritant particles that arrive after have nowhere to land and drift past.
> *Evidence:* Demonstrated — [study, year].

## Open on the baseline

Start with **the problem in the body** — the physiological state *without* the product. This is almost always the opening beat of the finished animation, and it's the beat teams most often under-brief. The audience has to feel the problem before the product means anything.

Describe what's going wrong, where, and what it looks like. Same four elements as any beat.

## Then: what the body does with the product

Separate from the mechanism itself. Cover, in plain terms:

- **Getting in** — how and where it's absorbed, and how long that takes.
- **Getting around** — where it travels and where it concentrates.
- **Timing** — onset, peak effect, duration. Use real numbers when the evidence supports them; animators use these to pace a sequence and writers use them for claims.
- **Getting out** — how it's metabolized and cleared.

Timing numbers are the single most useful thing you can give an animator about pacing. If the evidence has them, include them. If it doesn't, say so rather than estimating.

## Scale and camera

For each beat, state the scale it lives at and where the viewer sits:

- **Molecular** — individual molecules, receptors, binding.
- **Cellular** — cells, membranes, transport.
- **Tissue** — a lining, a surface, a structure.
- **Whole-body** — organ systems, a person.

Also note **scale transitions** — the moments the animation has to travel between levels (e.g., whole-body → tissue → molecular). These are the hardest shots to design and the most valuable to flag early.

## The honesty section

Close with **what the science does not say.** For a team about to make confident visual claims, this is the most protective slide in the deck:

- Mechanism steps that are plausible but not demonstrated.
- Findings from animal or in-vitro work not confirmed in humans.
- Effects shown at doses different from the product's.
- Anything the client's regulatory class prohibits depicting.

Say plainly what must not be visualized as settled fact. It's cheaper to constrain a concept now than to redo an animatic after MLR.

## Language conversions

Translate every time. Keep the technical term in parentheses on first mention only if the team will encounter it in client documents.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| Competitively inhibits the receptor | Blocks the receptor by occupying it first, so the irritant has nowhere to bind |
| Upregulates expression | The cell starts producing more of it |
| Increases mucosal hydration | Draws water into the lining, so it goes from dry and tight to soft and cushioned |
| Reduces inflammatory cascade | Interrupts the chain reaction that keeps the swelling going |
| Bioavailability of 85% | About 85% of the dose reaches the bloodstream |

## A note on the video companion

If a narration script is requested alongside the deck, follow the same beat order and keep every constraint above. Spoken language, short sentences, one visual cue in brackets per beat. It's a supplement — the written MOA slides remain the reference the team works from.
