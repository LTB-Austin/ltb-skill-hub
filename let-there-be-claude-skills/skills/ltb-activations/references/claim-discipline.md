# Claim discipline

The thing that makes an activation deck reviewable is that every line on every screen can be traced. Build the ID system first; everything else hangs off it.

## The four line types

Give every line one of these types. Prefixes can change per client; the four categories don't.

| Type | What it is | How it's used |
|---|---|---|
| **AC — approved claim** | Already cleared by MLR/LMR. Numbered to match the client's own approved list, so AC-8 is claim 8 on their sheet. | Verbatim, always, with its required disclaimer. Never reworded, never trimmed, never recombined. |
| **CX — context** | A supporting fact that makes no product claim (how the body works, what happens with age, what the category does). | Free to write, but it must be true and sourced. It is not a product claim and must not be positioned as one. |
| **IM — in-market** | Copy the brand already runs (an absorption claim, a cardiologist endorsement, a pack footnote). | Runs as it does today, with its current footnotes. Don't improve it without a stated reason. |
| **POS — positioning** | The theme line. | Positioning, not efficacy. It goes to MLR as a theme, never as a standalone efficacy statement. |

A fifth state, not a type: **PENDING MLR**. A line the science supports but the client hasn't cleared. Label it on the screen. Never mix it into a block of approved claims without the label.

## Numbering

- Match the client's own numbering wherever one exists. If their approved list is 1–9, your AC-1 through AC-9 are those nine, in that order. Say so in the legend.
- Keep IDs stable across the whole engagement — the Science Story deck, the activation deck, the handover. The client should be able to follow AC-7 from the evidence through to the PDP bullet.
- State in the footnote that these are working IDs until a formal Claim Set assigns them.

## What "verbatim" means

- Exact words, exact punctuation, including the asterisk.
- No splicing two approved claims into one sentence.
- No dropping a qualifier because it reads long. "Helps support" does not become "supports."
- You may put approved text inside a sentence of context copy, as long as the approved portion is unbroken and the surrounding words make no additional claim.

## Qualifiers that travel

Every claim carries its qualifiers wherever it goes. Carry them onto the activation screen, not just the substantiation appendix:

- **Dose disclosure** where the figure rests on a multi-unit dose.
- **Scope** where a claim belongs to one formulation only and cannot travel to a sibling SKU.
- **Endpoint honesty** — absorption is not relief; peak plasma is not onset.
- **Statistical honesty** — a median means half the group was sooner, and the footnote says so.
- **Non-disparagement** where the comparison is against the product's own components rather than a competitor.

## Visual guardrails are claims

A graphic can claim what the copy doesn't. Standing rules:

- No visual showing a measured level rising after taking the product unless a claim supports it.
- No visual showing the product acting inside the body in a way the claim doesn't state.
- No before/after of a person's symptoms.
- Where a current in-market graphic breaks one of these, flag it as REPLACE and say why.

## When something doesn't fit

If an activation needs a line the approved set doesn't contain, do not write it anyway. Options, in order:

1. Use an adjacent approved claim and adjust the creative around it.
2. Write it, label it PENDING MLR, and put it in its own block with the substantiation beside it.
3. Flag the gap to the user and let them decide whether to take it to the client.
